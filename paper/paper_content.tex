\begin{abstract}
Textual-program optimization must balance proposing and evaluating candidates
under a fixed rollout budget.  Evaluating each candidate more extensively
leaves fewer rollouts for proposing new candidates.  We introduce \method{},
which turns accumulated evaluation results into relative credit to guide
subsequent proposals.  On each
shared instance, attaining the highest observed score earns more credit when a
smaller fraction of the other candidates attain it.  \method{} averages these
per-instance credits to select candidates for further revision and updates the
credits as new results arrive.  For revision, it assigns batches of instances
using the selected candidates' score gaps to the best score recorded among them
on each instance.  Each new candidate is evaluated on instances that were not
used to generate it or its ancestors.  Across six benchmarks, \method{}
achieves the highest average score among the compared methods for both
Qwen3-8B and GPT-5.4-nano.  On Qwen3-8B, it improves over GEPA by $10.3$
percentage points on average and $31.3$ points on AIME-2025.  Ablations on
Qwen3-8B show that both relative credit and matching improve the average score
across the six benchmarks.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Recent work has shown that textual-program optimization can outperform
reinforcement learning with substantially fewer rollouts while keeping model
weights fixed \citep{gepa}.  This offers an efficient way to adapt language
models when task executions are expensive.  Evaluation cost has motivated
methods for selecting candidates under fixed budgets and for improving
performance per evaluation \citep{triple,kang2026textbo}.
The goal is to maximize the expected benchmark utility of the final program
under a limited rollout budget shared by candidate generation and evaluation.

Evaluating candidates more extensively leaves fewer rollouts for proposing new
ones.  The challenge is to use limited evaluations both to judge candidates and
to guide further proposals.  Rollouts provide traces for proposing revisions
\citep{cheng2024traceautodiffgenerativeoptimization} and scores for comparing
candidates.  The recorded scores can guide which candidates to revise and which
instances to use without additional evaluations for these decisions.
Comparisons on shared instances show where a candidate outperforms its peers or
falls behind.  New results can change these comparisons even when earlier
scores remain fixed.

\method{} (\textbf{C}redit-Aware \textbf{O}ptimization with \textbf{M}ini-batch
\textbf{P}roposal and \textbf{A}dmission for \textbf{S}kill \textbf{S}earch)
selects the candidates with the highest relative credit for further revision.
On each shared instance, attaining the highest score earns more per-instance
credit when a smaller fraction of the other candidates attain it.  COMPASS
averages these per-instance credits and updates relative credit as new results
arrive.  Proposal matching assigns batches that address each selected
candidate's weaknesses, using recorded score gaps between candidates on the
same instances.  Several candidates may favor the same batch, so the batches
are assigned jointly by maximum-weight matching.

\begin{samepage}
Each proposed candidate is evaluated on a separate admission batch to decide
whether it enters the candidate pool.  This batch excludes the instances used
to generate the candidate or its ancestors, because the candidate can inherit
changes made using those instances.  Accepted candidates join the pool, and
their admission results inform subsequent comparisons and proposal matching.
Figure~\ref{fig:overview} follows this process from candidate generation to
final selection.
\end{samepage}

We evaluate COMPASS on six benchmarks with Qwen3-8B and GPT-5.4-nano.  COMPASS
achieves the highest six-task aggregate among the compared methods for both
models.  On Qwen3-8B, the programs selected by COMPASS for each task reach $63.33\%$ on AIME-2025,
$43.37\%$ on IFBench, $69.53\%$ on LiveBench-Math, and $54.67\%$ on HoVer.
These scores exceed GEPA by $31.33$, $4.76$, $17.58$, and $2.34$
percentage points, respectively.  Qwen3-8B ablations show that relative credit
and proposal matching both improve the six-task aggregate.

This paper makes three contributions.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt,topsep=3pt]
  \item We introduce \method{} for textual-program optimization under a fixed
  rollout budget.  It combines relative credit for candidate selection,
  maximum-weight proposal matching, and paired admission outside each
  candidate's proposal history.

  \item We analyze paired admission and characterize when relative credit
  preserves sample-mean rankings.  We also derive a finite-pool regret bound
  for candidates selected by relative credit.

  \item Across six benchmarks, \method{} achieves the highest aggregate for both
  Qwen3-8B and GPT-5.4-nano.  Ablations on Qwen3-8B show aggregate gains from
  both relative credit and proposal matching.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Problem formulation}
\label{sec:setting}

\subsection{Budgeted textual-program optimization}

Given a seed textual program $p_0$, a benchmark evaluator, and a rollout
budget $B$, COMPASS generates and evaluates candidates, then returns one final
program.  Each attempt uses a proposal batch $P$ of
$b_P$ instances to generate a candidate and a separate admission batch $A$ of
$b_A$ instances to evaluate it.  Proposal generation and admission evaluation
share the rollout budget.  Proposal attempts are grouped into waves.
COMPASS starts a new wave while the cumulative
proposal and evaluation cost remains below $B$, then completes that wave before
stopping.

Let $\pi$ denote a complete run of proposal and admission decisions, and let
$\Pi(B,b_P,b_A)$ contain such runs with budget $B$ and batch sizes $b_P$ and
$b_A$.  Let
$p_{\mathrm{out}}(\pi)$ denote the final program returned by run $\pi$.
Let $R(p)$ denote the expected benchmark utility of program $p$, and let
$R^\star$ denote the best utility reachable under the search model.  The
budgeted optimization problem is
\[
\pi^\star\in
\arg\min_{\pi\in\Pi(B,b_P,b_A)}
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
R^\star-R\!\left(p_{\mathrm{out}}(\pi)\right)
\right].
\]
After selection, the returned program is evaluated on an independent frozen
test.

\subsection{Separating proposal and admission evidence}

Admission excludes the proposal batches used to generate a candidate or its
ancestors, because the candidate can inherit changes made using those batches.

Each accepted candidate after $p_0$ records its parent and proposal batch.  Let
$P_a^{\mathrm{birth}}$ be the proposal batch that created candidate $a$, with
$P_0^{\mathrm{birth}}=\varnothing$, and let $\operatorname{Anc}(s)$ contain all
ancestors of $s$.  The proposal history of candidate $s$ is
\[
\mathcal B_s=\bigcup_{a\in\operatorname{Anc}(s)\cup\{s\}}
P_a^{\mathrm{birth}}.
\]
A child proposed from parent $s$ on batch $P$ inherits $\mathcal B_s$ and adds
$P$, giving
\begin{equation}
X(s,P)=\mathcal B_s\cup P.
\label{eq:recursive_exclusion}
\end{equation}
Its admission batch satisfies $A\cap X(s,P)=\varnothing$.  If accepted, the
child records $X(s,P)$ as its proposal history.

\subsection{Reusing execution results}

COMPASS uses earlier execution results to select proposal parents and assign
proposal batches.  Executing program $p_s$ on an optimization instance $x$
yields a score in $[0,1]$, an output, and an execution trace.  At step $t$, the
cache retains one record for each observed
candidate--instance pair.  The score $q_t(s,x)$ is the highest valid, finite
score observed for that pair, and unevaluated pairs remain missing.

Relative credit also uses only observations outside a candidate's proposal
history.  The instances with eligible observations form the set
\[
\Omega_t(s)=\{x\mid q_t(s,x)\text{ exists and }x\notin\mathcal B_s\}.
\]
Relative credit guides parent selection.  Proposal matching uses cached scores,
including those on instances in a parent's proposal history, to identify where
that parent falls behind other candidates.

\section{Candidate Selection, Matching, and Admission in \method{}}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Relative credit from shared observations}
\label{sec:global_credit}

On a shared instance, a candidate receives more per-instance credit for attaining
the highest score when a smaller fraction of the other candidates attain it.
For example, if three candidates all succeed or all fail on a binary-scored
instance, each receives zero credit.  A single success gives credits
$(1,-\tfrac12,-\tfrac12)$. Two successes give
$(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,-1)$, with successful candidates listed first.

Let $\F_t(x)$ be the \emph{frontier}.  It contains the candidates with the
highest cached score among those for which $x\in\Omega_t(s)$, including all
ties.
In the all-failure example above, every candidate is on the frontier and each
receives zero per-instance credit.  Define the comparison set, its size,
frontier membership, and the frontier size by
\[
\mathcal C_t(x)=\{u\mid x\in\Omega_t(u)\},\qquad
N_x=|\mathcal C_t(x)|,\qquad
y_{sx}=\ind\{s\in\F_t(x)\},\qquad
K_x=\sum_{u\in\mathcal C_t(x)}y_{ux}.
\]
Per-instance credit subtracts the fraction of other candidates on the frontier
from the candidate's own frontier indicator.  When $N_x\ge2$, this gives
\[
r_{sx}=y_{sx}-\frac{K_x-y_{sx}}{N_x-1}
=\frac{N_xy_{sx}-K_x}{N_x-1}.
\]
COMPASS averages per-instance credit over instances with at least two eligible
observations to obtain relative credit $G_s$.
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\Omega_t^{\mathrm{cmp}}(s)&=\{x\in\Omega_t(s)\mid N_x\ge2\},
&E_s^{\mathrm{cmp}}&=|\Omega_t^{\mathrm{cmp}}(s)|,\\
R_s&=\sum_{x\in\Omega_t^{\mathrm{cmp}}(s)}r_{sx},
&G_s&=\frac{R_s}{E_s^{\mathrm{cmp}}}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:global_key}
\end{equation}
An observation with $N_x=1$ remains in the cache and begins contributing relative
credit when another candidate has an eligible observation on that instance.
The relative credit $G_s$ is undefined when $E_s^{\mathrm{cmp}}=0$.

New comparisons can change relative credit even when existing cached scores do
not change.  Whenever a cache update changes an observation or a frontier,
COMPASS recomputes $N_x$, $K_x$, all affected per-instance credits, and the
resulting $G_s$ values.

\subsection{A credit-ranked active set}
\label{sec:lineage_compression}

At each wave, COMPASS ranks all accepted candidates with defined relative credit
to select proposal parents.  The active set $\A_t$ contains the highest-credit candidates up to the
active-rank cutoff $\rho$, including every candidate tied at the cutoff.  Parents
and descendants are considered together.  If fewer than $\rho$ candidates have defined credit,
all of them remain active.  The tie rule can therefore leave more than $\rho$
active programs, and a parent and its descendant can remain active at the same
time.  If no candidate has defined credit, COMPASS uses the seed.

Proposal parents are selected from the active set in decreasing $G_s$ order,
with candidate index breaking exact ties.  The order is traversed cyclically
across waves, and each proposal slot defines one revision attempt.

\subsection{Proposal matching and admission}
\label{sec:proposal_allocation}
\label{sec:admission_allocation}
\label{sec:commit}

COMPASS assigns proposal batches to address each parent's weaknesses.  Higher
cached scores from other active candidates on the same instances indicate where
to focus revision.  The matching weight $H(s,P)$
averages $s$'s gap to the highest cached score among active candidates,
computed separately for each instance in $P$.
\begin{equation}
H(s,P)=\frac{1}{|P|}\sum_{x\in P}
\begin{cases}
\left[\max_{\substack{r\in\A_t\\q_t(r,x)\ \mathrm{finite}}}q_t(r,x)-q_t(s,x)\right]_+,
& q_t(s,x)\ \mathrm{finite},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:repairable_gap}
\end{equation}
Here $[v]_+=\max\{v,0\}$.  Missing parent observations contribute zero to this
matching weight and remain missing in the cache.

COMPASS draws $w$ proposal batches for the $w$ proposal slots.  Several slots
may favor the same batch, so COMPASS assigns the batches jointly.  For example,
two proposal slots may have the following matching weights.
\[
\begin{array}{c|cc}
 & P_1 & P_2\\ \hline
a & 0.40 & 0.30\\
b & 0.35 & 0.05
\end{array}
\]
Both prefer $P_1$.  Assigning $P_1$ to $a$ leaves $P_2$ for $b$, for a total
weight of $0.45$.  Assigning $P_2$ to $a$ and $P_1$ to $b$ gives $0.65$.

COMPASS therefore solves the maximum-weight matching problem over all proposal
slots and batches.  Let $\mathcal I_w=\{0,\ldots,w-1\}$, with $z_i$ denoting
the parent of slot $i$ and $P_j$ the $j$-th proposal batch.
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
Y^\star\in\argmax_{Y\in\{0,1\}^{w\times w}}\quad
&\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal I_w}H(z_i,P_j)y_{ij}\\
\text{s.t.}\quad
&\sum_{j\in\mathcal I_w}y_{ij}=1\ \ \forall i,
\qquad
\sum_{i\in\mathcal I_w}y_{ij}=1\ \ \forall j.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:proposal_assignment}
\end{equation}
Distinct slots receive distinct proposal batches even when they have the same
parent.

Each assigned parent is evaluated on its proposal batch, updating its cached
observations and providing execution traces for revision.  Proposal tasks that
proceed to child generation receive an admission batch and fixed references for
comparison.

For such a task with parent $s_i$ and proposal batch $P_i$, the evaluator
independently samples its admission batch $A_i$ outside $X(s_i,P_i)$.
On each admission instance $x$, it selects a current frontier candidate with
the largest defined relative credit as reference, using the parent if no frontier candidate has defined
credit.  Denote this reference by $r_i(x)$.  Missing reference observations are
evaluated and stored in the cache.  The evaluator fixes the admission batch,
references, and reference scores before generation and keeps them hidden from
the generator.

Once the generator fixes child $p_i$, the evaluator scores it on $A_i$.  The
child is admitted when its total score exceeds that of the fixed references.  Let
$q(p_i,x)$ be the child's score and $q(r_i(x),x)$ the fixed reference score on
the same instance.  The admission rule is
\begin{equation}
\Delta_i=\sum_{x\in A_i}\left[q(p_i,x)-q(r_i(x),x)\right],
\qquad
\operatorname{admit}(p_i)\iff\Delta_i>0.
\label{eq:admission_gate}
\end{equation}

An accepted child enters the candidate pool with its proposal history, and its
admission observations enter the cache.  COMPASS then recomputes the affected
$G_s$ values and updates the active set and queue.  At the budget boundary,
COMPASS returns the program with the largest defined $G_s$, with earliest index
breaking an exact tie.  Expanded execution details appear in
\Cref{app:algorithm}.

\section{Analysis of admission, credit, and final selection}
\label{sec:mechanism}

\subsection{Proposal history and paired admission}

A candidate that succeeds on every instance in its proposal batch and fails
elsewhere will score perfectly when evaluated on that batch again.  Its expected
benchmark utility can still be arbitrarily small.

\begin{proposition}[Reusing proposal examples can overstate performance]
\label{prop:self_cert_leakage}
For every admission size $n\ge1$ and $\epsilon>0$, there is a task
distribution and a proposal rule that observes $n$ examples, constructs a
program, and scores one when those examples are reused for evaluation,
although the program's population utility is at most $\epsilon$.
\end{proposition}

The construction in \Cref{app:proofs} uses memorization.  A descendant can
inherit this behavior, which explains why admission excludes the full proposal
history.

Admission in \Cref{eq:admission_gate} uses the total paired score difference
between a fixed child and its references.  Averaging these differences over the
admission batch preserves the sign and hence the admission decision.
\Cref{thm:paired_admission} bounds the deviation of this mean paired difference
from the average conditional mean.  At a fixed confidence level, the bound
decreases with admission batch size.

\subsection{Relative credit}

Paired admission decides whether a child enters the candidate pool.  Relative
credit ranks the accepted candidates for further revision and final selection.
Per-instance credit rewards candidates that reach the frontier when others do
not.  The positive credit they receive is exactly balanced by the negative
credit assigned to the remaining candidates.

\begin{proposition}[Instance-wise credit conservation]
\label{prop:credit_conservation}
For every instance $x$ with $N_x\ge2$, the per-instance credits defined immediately
before \Cref{eq:global_key} satisfy
\[
\sum_{s\in\mathcal C_t(x)}r_{sx}=0.
\]
\end{proposition}

With complete shared binary observations and one score per
candidate--instance pair, relative credit preserves the sample-mean ranking
and ties.  On each instance, per-instance credit equals the candidate's score
minus the mean score of the other candidates.  Averaging over the shared
instances gives its sample mean minus the average sample mean of the other
candidates.  Equivalently, every candidate's sample mean undergoes the same
centering and positive scaling (\Cref{eq:binary_credit_mean}).

\subsection{Search and final selection}
\label{sec:search_selection}

A search can miss a strong program or discover one and return a weaker
candidate.  The search gap and selection gap separate these two sources of
regret.  Let $\Pcal_B$ be the candidates available when search stops and let
$\hat p_B$ be the program selected by the largest defined $G_s$.  Its regret
has the exact decomposition
\begin{equation}
R^\star-R(\hat p_B)
=
\underbrace{R^\star-\max_{p\in\Pcal_B}R(p)}_{\text{search gap}}
+
\underbrace{\max_{p\in\Pcal_B}R(p)-R(\hat p_B)}_{\text{selection gap}}.
\label{eq:terminal_regret_decomposition}
\end{equation}
Relative credit and proposal matching guide candidate generation, while paired
admission determines which candidates enter the pool.  The resulting pool
determines the search gap, and the final choice within it determines the
selection gap.

To bound the selection gap, consider $M$ candidates, each fixed before receiving
$n$ conditionally independent and identically distributed rewards under a
common benchmark distribution, with mean $R(p_i)$.  Let $\widehat R_n(p_i)$ be the
arithmetic mean of these rewards and let $\widehat p_G$ maximize defined relative
credit in this pool.

The proof of \Cref{prop:bandwidth_precision} bounds all sample-mean errors
simultaneously.  The error bounds for the candidate with the highest expected utility
and the selected candidate give the selection-noise term.  For selection by
relative credit, the bound also includes the difference between the highest
sample mean and that of $\widehat p_G$,
\begin{equation}
\xi_G=\max_{i\le M}\widehat R_n(p_i)-\widehat R_n(\widehat p_G).
\label{eq:g_selection_gap}
\end{equation}
Under these evaluation conditions, \Cref{cor:g_selection} gives, with
probability at least $1-\delta$,
\begin{equation}
R^\star-R(\widehat p_G)
\le \underbrace{R^\star-\max_{i\le M}R(p_i)}_{\text{search gap}}
+\underbrace{2\sqrt{\frac{\log(2M/\delta)}{2n}}+\xi_G}_{\text{selection gap bound}}.
\label{eq:g_selection_regret}
\end{equation}
With $M$ and the confidence level fixed, increasing $n$ reduces the
selection-noise term.  With the shared binary observations considered above,
$\xi_G=0$.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Reflective textual-program optimization.}
Reflexion and Self-Refine use language feedback to revise behavior, while
TextGrad, Trace/OptoPrime, MIPROv2, and GEPA optimize textual components from
execution evidence
\citep{reflexion,self_refine,textgrad,
cheng2024traceautodiffgenerativeoptimization,miprov2,gepa}.  COMPASS uses the
resulting scores to choose which candidates to revise next and which batches
to assign them.

\paragraph{Evolution, illumination, and skill search.}
Evolutionary prompt and skill methods maintain multiple candidates to avoid a
single greedy trajectory~\citep{gepa,skillpro,grasp,evoskills,metaskillevolve}.
COMPASS keeps parents and descendants in one credit-ranked candidate pool.
Its active set contains the highest-credit candidates, including all ties at
the cutoff.

\paragraph{Adaptive evaluation and budget allocation.}
TRIPLE, POES, and Bayesian-Agent allocate evaluation or retrieval effort under
limited budgets~\citep{triple,poes,bayesianagent}. TextBO and APEX optimize
evaluation efficiency and development-example choice
\citep{kang2026textbo,wang2026apex}.  COMPASS allocates proposal attempts by
relative credit and assigns their batches jointly by maximum-weight matching.
Both decisions use the existing execution records.

\paragraph{Generalization after adaptive search.}
Repeated adaptive use of optimization data can widen the gap between selected
validation performance and held-out test performance
\citep{dwork2015generalization}.  GEPA addresses this empirically through
reusable rules and branch diversity~\citep{gepa}.  COMPASS carries proposal
history through the lineage.  Examples used to create an ancestor remain
excluded from a descendant's admission.  Held-out benchmark tests evaluate
the selected program.

\section{Conclusion}

COMPASS uses execution results to decide which candidates to develop and which
batches they should work on.  Proposal history determines the eligible
comparisons, and new results update the relative credit of candidates already
in the pool.  Relative credit selects proposal parents, and maximum-weight
matching assigns their batches.  Paired admission evaluates the resulting
revisions on separate examples.  Under the reported budgets, COMPASS achieves the highest six-task
aggregate for both Qwen3-8B and GPT-5.4-nano, and leads on all six Qwen3-8B tasks.
